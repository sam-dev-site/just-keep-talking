## When new voice input arrives

Do this in order, in the same task that handles the input:

1. **Save the source.** Put the raw material under `intake/owner-voice/` (or the relevant program-update folder). Prefer dated filenames. Do not rewrite the archive copy into “better” English.
2. **Refine this guide.** Add or adjust sections below. Quote short examples from the new material when they teach something the old guide missed. Remove or soften a rule if her new wording clearly contradicts it.
3. **Record the update.** Note in `project/STATUS.md` what was added to the guide and which source file it came from.
4. **Then apply to the site.** Only after the guide reflects the new input, update visitor-facing strings that the input affects. Keep her words; do not LLM-polish them.

## Who is speaking

Just Keep Talking should sound like a real person who cares about language learning, understands the challenges students face, and knows how to help. The overall feeling: a knowledgeable, friendly language teacher in a genuine conversation, not a company pushing a purchase.

Audrey founded the academy. Her personal voice (reflective, curious, occasionally funny, rooted in living between English and Spanish, Costa Rica and the United States) and the academy voice (still warm, a bit more structured and service-focused) should feel like one personality, not two brands.

## Brand personality

- Warm and welcoming: friendly and encouraging, not overly sentimental.
- Conversational and natural: write the way a thoughtful, knowledgeable person would speak.
- Knowledgeable but accessible: show teaching expertise without sounding academic, technical, or intimidating.
- Authentic and personal: real experiences and observations beat generic motivation.
- Thoughtful and observant: notice small, relatable things about language, culture, and learning.
- Confident but not pushy: believe in the methodology without exaggerating or constantly selling.

## Central message

**Learn a language by using it.**

Language is more than grammar rules, vocabulary lists, or passing exams. It is about connecting with people, expressing yourself, sharing experiences, and joining real conversations.

Site and marketing copy should keep reflecting:

- Conversation is at the center, supported by structure, guidance, and meaningful feedback.
- Every student has different goals, challenges, interests, and experiences. Learning should reflect that.
- Mistakes are a natural and necessary part of learning.
- Confidence grows through practice, encouragement, and experience.
- Language learning is about more than fluency: connection, independence, culture, and expressing who you are.

Do not treat grammar as the enemy, and do not suggest students learn by chatting alone. Balance communication, accuracy, structure, and personalized support.

## Website voice (this repo)

The public site should be warm, clear, and professionally confident. Explain the approach, programs, certified teachers, personalized plans, and student experience in clear, meaningful language.

Visitors should leave thinking: they understand what I need, they know what they are doing, and I would feel comfortable learning here.

- Prefer “Just Keep Talking,” “we,” and “our.” Do not use the abbreviation “JKT” in visitor-facing copy.
- Academy voice on service pages. Our Story uses the third-person owner paste in `intake/owner-voice/2026-10-23-audrey-our-story.md`. Keep first-person bios where she (or teachers) supplied them that way.
- Gentle, relevant invitations (WhatsApp). No aggressive sales language.

Other channels (for when content is drafted outside the site): Instagram Reels personal and natural; carousels thoughtful and useful; Stories casual and human; WhatsApp short and friendly; formal email/announcements organized and reassuring. Personality stays the same; presentation changes.

## How we write

Do more of:

- Natural, everyday language
- Specific examples and real situations
- Thoughtful observations
- Short, clear sentences
- Genuine questions
- Encouragement grounded in reality
- Stories and personal experiences
- Gentle, relevant invitations

Avoid:

- Corporate or overly polished language
- Vague motivational statements
- Generic language-learning tips
- Unnecessarily long explanations
- Forced engagement tactics
- Exaggerated promises
- Manufactured testimonials or fake “real” scenarios
- Aggressive sales language

### Tone examples (from the brand guide)

Too generic / salesy: “Unlock your full potential and master English with our innovative methodology!”

More like Just Keep Talking: “You might understand a lot of English, but when it's time to speak, finding the words can feel completely different. That's something we work on together.”

Too cheesy: “Don't let fear stop you from achieving your dreams! Your fluency journey starts today!” plus hype emojis.

More like Just Keep Talking: “You don't need to speak perfectly to have a good conversation. Sometimes the most important thing is being willing to try.”

## Preserve owner wording

When the owner supplies text, keep her words. You may fix clear typos (for example `hope in the car` → `hop in the car`), trim length for layout, or adapt for Spanish. Do not “upgrade” the phrasing into marketing copy.

If a draft and an owner paste conflict, the owner paste wins.

## Ban these AI habits

Do not introduce these patterns when writing or rewriting site copy:

1. Em dashes (`—`) and en dashes used as sentence pivots. Use a period, comma, colon, or parentheses instead.
2. Antithesis formulas invented for polish: stacked “not X, but Y”; “From X to Y” transformation headlines; “Knowing X is only the beginning.”
3. Staccato slogan stacks: “A is B. C is D. E is F.”
4. Empty intensifiers and hype: unlock, master (as sales verb), innovative methodology, seamless, elevate, journey (as metaphor), dive into, at the heart of, curated, crafted, fluency journey, rocket/sparkle emoji spam.
5. Third-person polishing of bios or founder story she wrote in first person.

Natural contrast that sounds like a teacher (“you might understand a lot… when it's time to speak…”) is fine when it matches her guide examples. Model-invented antithesis is not.

## Spanish

For Spanish content, especially for a Costa Rican audience:

- Use natural, friendly Costa Rican Spanish, generally with **usted**.
- Adapt so it sounds natural in Spanish; do not translate English word for word.
- Keep the same warmth, clarity, and conversational feeling in both languages.
- Recognize regional and cultural differences without treating one variety as universally correct.
- Use bilingual comparisons only when they offer a real insight.

Classes and Approach already use usted. Prefer usted on other public pages unless a specific owner paste is clearly tú.

## What people should feel

1. **Understood:** they get why language learning is hard and what I am experiencing.
2. **Confident in our expertise:** experienced teachers who know how learning works and can help me progress.
3. **Comfortable and welcome:** a place to practice, make mistakes, ask questions, and be myself.

## Guiding checks before shipping copy

- Does this sound like something we would genuinely say to a student or someone interested in language learning?
- Is this helping someone understand something, feel connected, learn something useful, or get to know us better?
- Could this sentence appear in a polite WhatsApp message from Audrey to a parent or adult student?
- Did an owner paste get rewritten into something “smoother”?
- Any em dash, hype verb, or clever From/to headline that was not in the owner text?
- Are bios still in the first-person voice where she supplied them that way? Does Our Story still match `intake/owner-voice/2026-10-23-audrey-our-story.md` (third person)?
- Did we avoid treating grammar as the enemy or learning as “just chat”?
